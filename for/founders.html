The product works. Not enough people know it exists.

You spent a year getting the thing right. You launched, posted about it, told everyone you know. And then the signups came in at a trickle, or not at all. Plenty of founders launch to zero paying customers and decide, months later, that distribution was the real problem all along. It usually is.

That’s the first gap. The second one shows up later, and it’s sneakier.

No distribution yet

Building felt like progress because it was. Getting the first 100 customers is a different skill, and nobody on the team owns it. So marketing turns into random acts: a launch post here, an ad test that ran for nine days. Nothing compounds because nothing repeats.

Founder-led sales hits a ceiling

You close most deals yourself, and you’re good at it. SaaStr puts the breaking point at the moment you personally close 70–80% of new business. The company grows only when you get on the call. And none of it is written down, so you can’t hand it to a hire yet.

Maybe you tried an agency to fix it. You got a dashboard and a monthly deck. What you couldn’t get was one customer you could point to. That isn’t bad luck. Cervin Ventures describes the agency setup for early-stage startups as “structurally set up to produce the wrong outcomes,” because agencies get paid for what’s easy to measure. Some agency contracts lock in ad spend before anyone knows which channel works.

Often the growth problem is a positioning problem. If the messaging is fuzzy, sales guesses, and nobody on the team agrees on who the product is for. More spend just makes that louder.

Positioning problems love to dress up as growth problems.

You know the mood. If you’ve pitched at a gBETA Indiana night, sat in a High Alpha office, or read TechPoint’s Indiana Venture Report, you’ve heard the word careful a lot. Investors here want to see efficient growth. We aren’t affiliated with any of those groups. We just work down the road from them, and we price for founders who are watching every dollar. Not in Indiana? Founder calls happen over video anyway.

You’re still finding the fit

  • Talk to 20 people who have the problem, and write down their exact words.
  • Sell the first ten customers by hand. No ad budget yet.
  • Write one sentence on who it’s for, and keep rewriting it until a stranger gets it.

What founders ask us first

Short answers, so the call can be about your product.

What does a fractional CMO cost for a startup?

Ours runs $1,200, $2,400, or $4,800 a month, with a three-month minimum and then month to month. We’d point most founders to the $3,000 strategy engagement first, so the retainer has a plan to run. For comparison, 2026 pricing guides from MarketerHire and GoFractional put fractional CMO retainers at $3,000 to $15,000 monthly. Ad spend, if you run ads, goes on your own card.

Are we too early to hire a marketing consultant?

Maybe. If you don’t have paying customers who stay yet, you’re probably pre-product-market fit, and a retainer would waste your runway. Book the call anyway. We’ll say so plainly and send you off with homework: customer interviews, a few hand-sold deals, and one sentence on who it’s for. Come back when people pay and stick around.

Should we hire a full-time marketer instead?

Eventually, yes. Right now a full-time hire costs more than any of our tiers and still needs someone to tell them what to do. The first marketing hire usually fails because nothing was written down before they arrived. We write the playbook with you, run it until it works, and hand it to your hire when you make one.

We got burned by an agency. How is this different?

An agency gets paid for deliverables: posts, ads, a monthly deck. We’re measured on customers and pipeline, and the monthly report says how many came from where. We cap the whole consultancy at eight clients, so the people on your call know your product. Your accounts stay in your name, and after three months you can stop any month.

Do you work with founders outside Indianapolis?

Yes. We’re based in Indianapolis, and the strategy, fractional CMO, and technical work all run over video, so founders anywhere in the U.S. work with us the same way. If you’re local, we’re happy to meet in person too.
