The $10,000 a month your nonprofit isn’t spending

Google sets aside free search ads for verified nonprofits every month. Here’s who qualifies and what gets accounts shut off. Then an honest look at who should run yours.

By Carson Poore Last updated: October 2026

Through its Ad Grants program, Google gives verified 501(c)(3) nonprofits up to $10,000 a month in free Google Search ads, with no cap on how many organizations take part. Most self-managed accounts use a few hundred dollars of it. Ad Grants agencies put the average near $300. The rest doesn’t roll over.

None of that says anything bad about the people running those accounts. The grant comes wrapped in rules that almost nobody explains clearly, and the person who set yours up may have left two development directors ago. So here are the rules in plain words, plus a straight answer on whether to keep running it yourself.

What exactly is the Google Ad Grant?

It’s advertising credit. Google funds text ads that appear on its search results when someone types something close to your work, like “volunteer opportunities in Indianapolis” or “free tax help near me.” You never get a bill for grant spending.

There are limits worth knowing up front. Grant ads run on Google Search only, as text. No YouTube, no banner ads. The budget resets monthly, so an unused month is simply gone. And grant ads sit behind paying advertisers in the auction, which is part of why $10,000 is harder to spend than it sounds.

Who qualifies, and how do you apply?

You need current 501(c)(3) status in the United States and a working website you own, with real content about your mission and a secure (https) address. Government agencies, hospitals and health systems, schools, and universities are excluded, though a university’s separate philanthropic arm can sometimes qualify.

The application runs in this order:

  1. Create a Google for Nonprofits account and request verification of your organization.
  2. Accept Google’s nonprofit certifications, which cover non-discrimination and how donations are used.
  3. Once verified, apply for Ad Grants from inside that account. Google reviews your website at this stage.
  4. Build the ad account to the program’s rules before you switch anything on. Most early suspensions trace back to this step.

Faith-based organizations deserve a straight answer here, so: it depends. Many qualify. Some don’t, based on how they’re registered and verified and on what the ads would promote. We check eligibility case by case rather than promise it on a web page.

So why do most accounts spend so little?

If you’ve ever said “we have the grant, but nobody’s touched it in months,” you have a lot of company. The usual story goes like this. Someone sets the account up during a busy season, often a volunteer or a staffer who has since moved on. They pick a handful of keywords built around the organization’s own name. Those keywords get great click rates, because the only people searching your name already know you. The account stays compliant and barely spends.

That’s the quiet trap. The safest-feeling setup is also the one that reaches almost no new people. Spending more means writing ads for the searches strangers actually make, and those ads need tending, because the rules punish an account that drifts.

What gets an Ad Grant account suspended?

Three requirements cause most of the trouble.

What is the 5% click-through rule?

For every 100 times your ads are shown, at least 5 people need to click, measured across the whole account each month. Fall under that line and stay there for consecutive months, and Google deactivates the account until you fix it and ask to be reinstated. Broad keywords that show often and earn few clicks are the usual cause.

Does someone really have to log in every month?

Yes. The program treats a quiet account as an abandoned one. Someone has to sign in monthly and keep making real changes, and the program also sends an annual survey that has to be completed. This is the rule that catches organizations after a staff change. The login belonged to whoever left.

What else trips accounts up?

Conversion tracking is required, so the account has to record something meaningful on your site, like a donation or a volunteer sign-up. Single-word keywords aren’t allowed outside a few exceptions such as your own name. Keywords Google rates as low quality have to go. None of it is hard. It just has to be somebody’s job.

A suspension is not a lost grant. Ads stop. You fix the problem and request reinstatement. Annoying, and fixable.

And not every organization should spend the full $10,000. If your site can’t take a donation or a volunteer sign-up cleanly, more clicks just mean more people leaving. Fix the site first.

Our take: if one person on your team has a few hours a month and actually enjoys this kind of puzzle, try running it yourself first. What you learn about how people search for your cause is worth keeping in-house. Bring in help when nobody owns the account or it’s been suspended. Where should the money actually go?

Most organizations point the grant at donations first and volunteers last. We think that’s backwards.

Volunteer searches are specific and full of intent, and they’re rarely the searches paying advertisers fight over. The need is real, too: most nonprofits call volunteers essential, and fewer Americans volunteer than before COVID.

Program awareness comes next. Someone searching for the help you provide is the person your mission exists for, and an ad that reaches them is program delivery, not overhead. That’s a sentence worth repeating to your board.

Donors still matter. Total giving has held up lately while the number of donors has fallen, so new donors are scarce everywhere. Search can find them. Expect small first gifts, and have an email thank-you ready, because what happens after the first gift decides whether there’s a second.

If paying for help is the sticking point in central Indiana, look at capacity money before your operating budget. United Way of Central Indiana awards capacity-building grants of up to $50,000, and capacity work like this is what that money exists for. Anything else people ask about the grant?

Is the Google Ad Grant really free?

Yes. Google funds the ads, and you never get a bill for grant spending. The real cost is time: someone has to follow the program rules and keep the account active, or Google pauses it.

Can a church or faith-based nonprofit get a Google Ad Grant?

Often, but not always. It depends on how the organization is registered and verified and on what the ads would promote. Google’s rules have real nuance here, so we check eligibility case by case instead of promising it up front.

What happens if our Ad Grant account gets suspended?

Your ads stop, but the grant isn’t gone. You fix whatever caused the suspension, usually the 5% click-through rule or inactivity, then request reinstatement from inside the account. The fix usually starts with cutting keywords that get shown a lot and clicked rarely.

Can Ad Grant ads run on YouTube or other websites?

No. Grant ads are text ads on Google Search results only. Video and display placements need a separate, paid Google Ads account.
