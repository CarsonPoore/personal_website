When should you hire a fractional CMO?

You built something people pay for. Now the question is who steers the marketing. Here are the signs you’re ready, the signs you’re early, and a stage-by-stage table... including the cases where we’d tell you to skip us.

By Carson Poore Last updated: Oct 9, 2026

Hire a fractional CMO when customers already buy, revenue is real (we say past roughly $500K), and nobody owns the marketing plan or its results. If people aren’t buying yet, wait. Below: the signs by stage, and the cases where we’d tell you to skip us.

Here at CPC, we aren’t afraid of a hot take, even about our own service. Don’t get us wrong, we sell this, so weigh our answer accordingly. But the honest version is narrower than most fractional CMO pages admit. Plenty of owners are ready. Plenty more need a different hire, or none at all.

What’s the real test for hiring a fractional CMO?

It comes down to 2 questions. Do customers already buy from you, again and again, without you personally closing every one? And can you name the person responsible for whether marketing works?

If the first answer is no, you don’t have a marketing problem yet. You have an offer or audience problem, and no retainer fixes that. If the first is yes and the second is no... you’re exactly the person this role exists for.

Revenue is a rough filter, not the test. MarketerHire’s guide to fractional CMO costs is written for companies with roughly $2M to $50M in revenue, because its retainers run $3,000 to $15,000 a month. Ours start at $1,200, so we start earlier. So price sets the entry point as much as company size does.

Which stage are you at?

Here’s how we’d sort it, with straight answers on what to do instead when the answer is no.

Stage What it looks like Hire one? Do this
Before product-market fit Sales are sporadic and you’re still changing the offer Not yet Sell it yourself and talk to customers. A one-time plan at most
Founder-led selling Customers love it, but every sale runs through you Plan first The $3,000 strategy engagement, then decide on a retainer
$500K to $2M, scattered A site, some ads, a newsletter, a freelancer, and nothing connecting them Yes A fractional CMO who also builds
Past $2M, with vendors An agency or two, reports nobody trusts, no one directing the work Yes Someone to set direction and challenge the vendors
One proven channel Google Ads pays back every time and you want more of it Probably not A specialist or agency for that channel
A big team and a board Marketing needs 40+ hours of leadership a week No A full-time CMO

That last row has a number attached. MarketerHire says to consider a full-time CMO when the work needs 40 or more hours a week, the marketing team passes about 10 people, or the board wants a dedicated executive. It puts the break-even at fees above $12,000 to $15,000 a month for 6 months or more. Most businesses we talk to sit nowhere near that line, and that’s fine.

What are the signs you’re ready?

5 show up again and again. If you nod at 3, keep reading.

  • You do a lot and can’t say which part brought in a customer. Posts. Ads. Emails. A new site. And no single number says whether any of it worked.
  • Your vendors don’t talk to each other. MarketerHire lists channels that work against each other, and agencies nobody manages well, among its signs. The website person, the ad person, and the social person each hit their own targets, and nobody owns the whole picture.
  • You keep buying tactics and canceling them. vcita’s April 2025 survey of 500 US small businesses found 40% of those that outsource marketing end up discontinuing or switching their agency. Lack of return was the reason 60% gave.
  • Every marketing decision waits for you, and you’d rather be doing the work the business sells.
  • A big decision is coming (an ad budget, a hire, a second location) and nobody can tell you what should come first.

Our hot take, stated plainly: the last sign is the best reason to hire, and the one owners wait longest on. Most owners bring in marketing leadership late, then ask it to make up the lost time in a quarter. In our book, earlier and smaller beats later and desperate.

Why not just hire a marketing manager?

You can. Just know the price first. The Bureau of Labor Statistics puts median pay for advertising, promotions, and marketing managers at $165,780 a year (May 2025). That’s salary only. No benefits, no software, and it’s a manager, not someone who’s run marketing through growth stages.

Even at the top, the seat turns over. Spencer Stuart’s annual study, reported by Marketing Week, found the average CMO at a Fortune 500 company stays about 4.3 years.

It’s not a manager vs. a CMO, it’s running marketing vs. deciding what marketing should be. That’s how we see it, and many small businesses need the second before they can afford the first. We cover the agency side of this choice in fractional CMO vs. marketing agency, and the do-it-yourself side in should you do your own marketing or hire someone.

When shouldn’t you hire one?

Before product-market fit. If people aren’t buying yet, the problem is the offer or the audience. We turn down retainer inquiries at that stage and send homework instead. We’d rather tell you here than on a call.

When you already have the judgment. Maybe you spent 10 years in marketing before you bought the business. Paying a second strategist to agree with the first is waste. Buy hands instead.

When one channel already works. Hire an agency or a good freelancer for that channel. It’s cheaper, and a better fit.

When the retainer would strain the business. If $1,200 a month squeezes you, you’re early, and that’s okay. Our free Budget Calculator will give you a defensible number to grow into.

One more test for anyone you do hire: ask who implements, by name. Many fractional CMOs hand you a plan without hands. Here at CPC, the same team that sets the direction builds the pages, automations, and ad accounts. Other good answers exist. No answer is a red flag.

What else do owners ask about hiring a fractional CMO?

How much revenue do you need before hiring a fractional CMO?

There’s no official line, so here’s ours. MarketerHire’s guide targets companies at roughly $2M to $50M in revenue. Ours is lower, around $500K, because our retainers start at $1,200 a month. Below that, honestly, a one-time plan you run yourself beats a retainer.

Is it too early to hire a fractional CMO for a startup?

If customers aren’t buying consistently yet, honestly, yes. Before product-market fit the problem is the offer or the audience, and a retainer can’t fix either. Sell it yourself, listen hard, and come back to us. But a founder with early repeat customers is a different story. Start with the $3,000 strategy engagement.

When should you switch from a fractional CMO to a full-time one?

MarketerHire’s guide suggests it when you need 40 or more hours a week of CMO-level work, your marketing team passes about 10 people, or your board wants a dedicated executive. It puts the break-even at fees above $12,000 to $15,000 a month for 6 months or more.

Should I hire a fractional CMO or a marketing agency first?

If nobody can say what marketing should accomplish this quarter, hire judgment first. If you know exactly what works and just can’t produce enough of it, hire hands. We wrote the full comparison up as Fractional CMO vs. marketing agency.
