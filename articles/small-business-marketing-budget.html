How much should a small business spend on marketing?

A sourced percentage, the dollar math at three revenue sizes, and our honest take on where the first dollars belong.

By Carson Poore Last updated: October 2026

The U.S. Small Business Administration’s guideline is to spend 7–8% of gross revenue on marketing if your business brings in less than $5M a year. For a $500K business, that’s about $2,900–$3,300 a month.

A fair warning before the math. A consultancy telling you how much to spend on marketing has an obvious stake in the answer. So none of the percentages on this page are ours. The opinions are, and we’ve labeled them as opinions.

Is 7–8% of revenue the right number for your business?

It’s a starting point. A blunt one. The SBA gives a single range for every business under $5M, which lumps a two-truck plumbing company in Greenwood together with a 30-person software firm in Fishers. Those two shouldn’t budget the same way.

Stage matters more than size. Published budget guides converge on roughly these bands:

  • 10–20%Getting started. You’re finding customers beyond friends and the first few referrals.
  • 7–10%Growing. Something works, and you’re adding crews or chairs to keep up with it.
  • 4–7%Steady. Established, with most of the work coming from repeat customers and word of mouth.

The top band looks alarming at first. Twenty percent of revenue? It makes more sense once you remember what a new business doesn’t have yet: reviews and a list of past customers to call in a slow month. You’re paying to build things an older business already owns.

If you land between two columns, use the lower number for your first three months. Raise it once you can see which spending brings in calls. That rule matters more than whether you pick 6% or 8%.

Where should the first marketing dollars go?

Here’s our opinion, plainly: most small businesses should spend their first dollars on things they’ll still own a year from now. Everything else in this section follows from that.

For a local business, that usually means your Google Business Profile and your reviews. BrightLocal’s local search research found that businesses in Google’s top three map results get 126% more traffic than the ones ranked four through ten. It also found that profiles with 40 or more reviews and a 4.5-star average show up in those top three about 3.7 times as often as profiles with fewer than 10. Neither one costs much beyond time and a system for asking.

Next, answer the phone. A lead you paid for and didn’t pick up is the most expensive marketing there is. If you’re on a roof or behind a line all day, an automatic text-back for missed calls is worth more than a bigger ad budget. Our page for local businesses lays out the order we’d work in.

What shouldn’t you buy yet?

Social media management, if your Google profile has eight reviews and photos from 2019. And Google Ads, if you can’t yet tell which calls came from an ad. Both are fine purchases later. Early, they’re where budgets leak.

Is it cheaper to rent leads or own your pipeline?

If you’re in the trades, you’ve met the rental option. Pay-per-lead platforms like Angi and HomeAdvisor sell you a lead at a time. The alternative is owning the pieces that bring work in on their own: your profile, your site, an ad account in your name, and a list of past customers.

Renting leads vs. owning your pipeline
Renting (Angi-style platforms) Owning (your profile, site, and ad account)
Cost per lead Higher; you pay for every lead, won or lost Lower once your profile and site are working
Who else gets the lead Often other contractors too; shared leads are a common complaint Nobody. The customer found you.
Billing Charges that keep coming after cancellation are a common complaint Ad spend goes to Google from your account, at a budget you set
When you stop paying The leads stop the same day Your reviews, ranking, and customer list stay

Renting isn’t always wrong. A new contractor with an empty calendar and no reviews can use platform leads to get through a first season while the owned pieces get built. The trouble shows up later, when the platform is still most of the pipeline. By then you’ve paid for hundreds of leads and own none of the things that produced them.

Questions owners ask about marketing budgets

Is 7–8% of revenue too much for a small business to spend on marketing?

It’s the SBA’s guideline for businesses under $5M, so it isn’t unusual. If margins are thin, start in the 4–7% band that published budget guides give established businesses, then raise it once you can see which spending brings in work.

Does a marketing budget include ad spend?

Yes. Count everything in one number: ad spend, agency or consultant fees, software, and print. A $1,200 retainer plus $800 a month in Google Ads is a $2,000-a-month marketing budget, and that’s the figure to hold against the percentage.

Should a new business spend more on marketing than an established one?

Usually, as a share of revenue. Published budget guides put early-stage businesses at roughly 10–20% of revenue and mature ones at 4–7%. A new business is paying to build the reviews and the customer list an older one already has.

How much should a $500K business spend on marketing per month?

About $2,900 to $3,300 a month at the SBA’s 7–8% guideline, or $35,000 to $40,000 a year. A newer $500K business can reasonably spend more. A steady one that runs mostly on repeat customers might sit closer to $1,700 to $2,900.

Are pay-per-lead sites like Angi worth the money?

Sometimes, early, when you need jobs this month and have no reviews yet. Over time they tend to cost more per lead than channels you own. And when you stop paying, the leads stop.
