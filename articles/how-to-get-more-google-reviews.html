How do I get more Google reviews without breaking the rules?

A routine that fits in a truck or behind a counter, the words to send, and the shortcuts Google will punish.

By Carson Poore Last updated: Oct 9, 2026

Text every customer your direct Google review link the day the job wraps. Ask everyone, offer nothing in return, and never screen out the unhappy ones. Those are Google’s own rules. Do it every time, and the count climbs steadily.

You’ve done good work for years, and your Google profile shows eleven reviews. That’s not a customer problem. Happy people rarely write reviews on their own. They write them when someone asks, and most owners ask in bursts, then forget. Below is the routine we’d set up for you, written so you can start it this week.

Why do reviews matter more this year than last?

Because shoppers got pickier. BrightLocal’s 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey found 68% of consumers will only use a business rated four stars or higher, up from 55% a year earlier. And 31% now want 4.5 or better, up from 17%.

Recency counts too. Same survey: 74% only care about reviews written in the last three months, and 47% won’t use a business with fewer than 20 reviews. A wall of five-star praise from 2021 doesn’t help you.

The cost of a thin profile is real. In BrightLocal’s 2026 Consumer Search Behavior study of 1,227 US consumers, 65% of the people who rejected a business were put off by something review-related, such as a low rating or old reviews.

One piece of good news. Only 10% of consumers say they’ll use a business only if it has five stars. You don’t need a perfect score. You need a steady, believable one.

What does Google allow when you ask?

We read Google’s Maps content policy and its review tips for businesses this month. The short version: you can ask. You can’t bribe, filter, or coach.

What you might do Allowed? Why
Text or email every customer a direct review link Yes Google tells businesses to remind customers with a link or QR code
Put a QR code on a receipt or counter sign Yes Google lists receipts, thank-you emails, and in-store displays
Reply publicly to every review Yes Google recommends it, as long as the reply isn’t a sales pitch
Ask only the customers you think are happy No The policy bars merchants from selectively soliciting positive reviews
Send unhappy customers to a private form, happy ones to Google No It’s still selective, because only the happy customers get the Google link
Offer a discount, gift card, or freebie for a review No Payment, discounts, and free goods or services count as fake engagement
Offer a refund if they remove a bad review No Incentives to revise or remove a review are banned too
Tell customers what to write, or set staff quotas No The policy names requests for specific content and staff targets

One gray area, and it’s our read, not Google’s wording: the policy says not to require or pressure people to review while they’re on your premises. A QR sign by the register is an invitation. An employee holding a tablet in front of a customer is pressure. Pick the sign.

Google isn’t the only one watching. The FTC’s 2024 rule on fake reviews bans incentives conditioned on a particular sentiment, positive or negative, and lets the agency seek civil penalties against knowing violators.

Break the rules and the result is usually quiet. Google removes the reviews, sometimes a lot of them at once, and can restrict your profile. You end up with fewer reviews than you started with.

The routine, with a text you can copy

Ask when the customer has just seen the result: the job is done, or the table has paid. BrightLocal’s 2026 survey found 78% of consumers were asked for a review in the past year, and 65% wrote one after being asked. Asking works. It also means your customers get a lot of requests, so keep yours short and personal.

Here’s the routine.

  1. Sign in at business.google.com on a computer, open Read reviews, choose Get more reviews, and copy your link. Google says QR codes can only be made on a computer, not a phone.
  2. Save the text below as a shortcut on your phone, with your link already in it.
  3. Send it to every customer the day the work wraps. Every one, including the ones you’re unsure about.
  4. If someone doesn’t respond, send one gentle reminder a few days later. Then stop.
  5. Reply to every review within a couple of days, good and bad.

Here’s a text you can copy and make yours.

Copy-ready text

Hi Maria, it’s Dave from Smith Plumbing. Thanks for having us out today. If you have a minute, an honest review on Google helps your neighbors find us: [your link]. No pressure either way. Dave

Look at what it leaves out. No “five stars,” no suggested wording. “Honest” is doing the work.

Will it feel awkward the first ten times? Yes. By the twentieth it’s like asking for the customer’s address.

How do you answer reviews, the rough ones too?

Quickly, and like a person. BrightLocal’s survey found 89% of consumers expect owners to respond to reviews, and 81% expect that response within a week. Only 19% want one the same day, so a couple of days is fine.

Google’s own advice is short and personal: use the customer’s name, mention their job, and leave the deals out. It also says to move complicated cases to a phone call or email and never to share a reviewer’s private details. Our own rule for a bad review: say sorry once, say what you’ll do, give a phone number, and stop. Don’t argue. The reply is public.

A fake or abusive review is different. Use Google’s report tool and let the review process work.

How many reviews is realistic?

Four a month gets you from zero to 20 in five months and to 40 in ten. That’s slower than you want and faster than it feels. A busy shop that asks everyone will do better. A shop that asks twice a year won’t.

We said steady for a reason. We think a trickle every week beats a big push every January. It matches what shoppers say they read, and it doesn’t look like a spike to Google’s spam filters, which watch for unusual volumes and patterns.

Who can skip the push for more reviews?

Not many, but a few. If you see fewer than ten customers a month, the routine still works. It’s just slow, so start by asking past customers too (that’s allowed) and put your effort into a finished profile.

If the work has real quality problems, fix those first. Reviews amplify what you do, good or bad. And if you don’t answer your phone, more reviews just mean more missed calls. Sort that out before anything else.

Last, skip anyone who sells “guaranteed five-star reviews.” Google removes them, and your profile takes the hit.

What else do owners ask about Google reviews?

Can I ask customers for Google reviews?

Yes. Google tells businesses to remind customers with a direct link or QR code. The line you can’t cross is how you ask: no incentives, no screening for happy customers, no telling people what to write.

Can I give a discount or gift card for a review?

No. Google’s policy bans payment, discounts, and free goods or services in exchange for a review, or for changing or removing one. The FTC’s 2024 rule also bans incentives conditioned on a particular sentiment. Say thank you in person instead.

Is it okay to send unhappy customers to a private feedback form first?

Not as a filter. Google prohibits selectively soliciting positive reviews or discouraging negative ones. Send everyone the same Google link. If you also want private feedback, collect it separately and don’t use it to decide who sees the Google link.

How many Google reviews do I need?

There’s no magic number. BrightLocal’s 2026 survey found 47% of consumers won’t use a business with fewer than 20 reviews, and 74% only care about reviews from the last three months. Aim for 20 or more, and keep a few coming in every month.

Why did some of my Google reviews disappear?

Google uses automated spam detection and removes reviews it flags, and it also removes reviews that break its policy. Bursts of reviews and reviews from employees or family are common triggers. Some genuine reviews get caught too. A slow, steady pace is the safest pattern.
