Website redesign or refresh: which does your site need?

The signals that tell you which one you have, what each costs in practice, and the honest case for fixing less than you think.

By Carson Poore Last updated: Oct 9, 2026

Refresh if your site still says what you do, works on a phone, and gets some inquiries: fix the words, photos, speed, and contact path. Redesign only when the foundation blocks you. You can’t edit it, it can’t load fast on a phone, or its structure no longer matches what you sell. Most small business sites need a refresh.

Here’s the catch with that answer. A dated-looking site feels like it needs a redesign. Usually it needs two afternoons and a better first sentence. Below is a way to tell the difference, with the signals in one table.

What’s the difference between a refresh and a redesign?

A refresh keeps the bones: same platform, same pages, same menu. You change what sits on them. The words, the photos, the speed, the form, the first screen a visitor sees.

A redesign changes the bones. New structure, new navigation, usually a new platform, and pages rewritten from scratch. A refresh is hours. A redesign is a project, with a quote and a launch date.

Which signals point to which?

Find the lines that sound like your site. If they all land in the middle column, refresh. If one or two land on the right, read that row twice.

The traffic rows matter most. A new design won’t bring visitors. If few people arrive, our guide to why a business isn’t showing up on Google comes first.

What do usability researchers say about redesigns?

They lean toward fixing less. The usability research firm Nielsen Norman Group warns that big redesigns can break things users rely on, cost a lot, and stall in debate. It lists a short set of cases where an overhaul is justified: small fixes have stopped helping, outdated technology blocks critical changes, the structure is a tangle, conversion is low across the whole site, or competitors are far ahead.

That list is a decent test. Notice that dated looks isn’t on it. It also names a bias worth knowing about: designers tend to favor visual overhauls over fixing content, structure, or forms. We sell websites, so take this as a confession. Sometimes the right answer is a smaller job than we’d like.

A refresh checklist, in order

Work down this list in order. Stop when the leaks stop.

  1. The first screen. Say what you do, for whom, and where, in one plain sentence. Nielsen Norman Group has found that users often leave a page within 10 to 20 seconds, and the odds of leaving are highest in the first 10.
  2. Phone speed. Google’s guidance is that a page’s largest element should load in 2.5 seconds or less, measured on mobile. Slower than that, and a visitor on parking-lot signal is gone before the page finishes. In our experience, giant photos are the usual cause.
  3. The contact path. A tap-to-call number and one clear button on the first screen. Then fill out your own form from your phone and see what happens.
  4. Photos and proof. Real photos of your work from this year. Recent reviews. A price or a price range.
  5. Dead weight. Delete pages nobody reads. Update hours, service areas, and the year in the footer.
  6. Accounts. Make sure the domain and hosting are in your name. If they aren’t, fix that before anything else. It’s the one item here that can cost you the whole site.

Speed deserves its own note. Unbounce’s 2024 benchmark for commercial and professional services landing pages found that mobile made up 81% of visits but converted at 8.3%, against 11.6% on desktop. Your own split will differ. But if your phone visitors convert worse than your desktop ones, the gap is often fixable without a redesign.

When is a redesign worth it?

When the site itself blocks you. You’ve changed what you sell, you can’t update it without calling someone, or the platform can’t be made fast. Then fixing around the edges wastes money.

Do it carefully. Write the words before the design, because that’s the slow part and the part that sells. Keep your old addresses working by redirecting every old page to its new home. Otherwise you lose the search visibility you’ve built. And build it in accounts you own, so a redesign never turns into a hostage situation. Our ownership page spells out what stays yours.

When is neither worth doing?

If referrals keep you booked, your site only has to confirm you exist. A one-page site and a finished Google profile may be plenty. The same goes if you can’t yet say in one sentence what you sell. A new design just puts the confusion online. Our website cost guide has a longer list under when a new website isn’t worth paying for.

What else do owners ask about redesigns?

How often should a small business redesign its website?

There’s no fixed schedule. Age matters less than signals: you can’t edit the site, it’s slow on phones and can’t be fixed, or its structure no longer matches what you sell. If none of those are true, refresh the words, photos, and speed instead of starting over.

How much does a website refresh cost?

It depends on what’s broken. On a site builder, much of a refresh is a few evenings of your own time. We don’t publish a refresh price, but on a free call we’ll look at your site and tell you whether it’s an afternoon of fixes or a rebuild.

Will a redesign hurt my Google rankings?

It can, if old pages disappear or their addresses change without redirects. Redirect every old page to its new home, keep the text that already ranks, and check Google Search Console for errors in the weeks after launch.

Can I refresh a Wix or Squarespace site myself?

Yes, in most cases. Rewrite the first screen, replace oversized photos, check the contact form, and cut pages nobody reads. Builders handle that well. Hire help when the site is slow or clumsy after you’ve done those basics.
