How do I optimize my Google Business Profile?

The checklist in the order that matters, with Google’s own limits, the mistakes that get profiles suspended, and what you can skip.

By Carson Poore Last updated: Oct 9, 2026

Pick the most specific primary category for what pays your bills, then fill in everything else: real hours, a service list, a description in your own words, an honest address or service area, and photos of your actual work. Post once a month and answer every review. It’s free and takes about an hour.

Plenty of owners claimed their profile years ago, typed in a phone number, and never came back. Fair enough. Nobody told you it was the front door. For a lot of local searches it is: the first thing people see about you is this box, not your website. This checklist takes you through it in the order we’d do it.

What decides who shows up on the map?

Google names three factors in its own guidance on local ranking: relevance, distance, and prominence. Relevance is how well your profile matches the search. Distance is how far you are from the person searching. Prominence is how well known you are, which Google ties to links from other sites and to your reviews and ratings. Google also says you can’t pay for a better local ranking.

You can’t move your shop closer to the searcher. Most of what you can change sits under relevance, and that’s this checklist. In Whitespark’s 2026 Local Search Ranking Factors survey, the primary category came in as the number one factor, and additional categories came in at number eight. Start there.

Completeness pays off with customers too. Google says people are 2.7 times more likely to consider a business reputable if they find a complete Business Profile, and 70% more likely to visit. The page doesn’t say how Google measured that, so treat the numbers as direction, not a promise.

The bar is low. BrightLocal’s 2025 small business report, a survey of 778 US owners and managers, found only 35% have a Google Business Profile at all. Finishing yours puts you ahead of most.

Which parts cost you customers when they’re wrong?

In BrightLocal’s 2026 Consumer Search Behavior study of 1,227 US consumers, consumers said what made them pass on a business. 20% cited missing or wrong business details. 18% couldn’t find a website. 16% saw no photos, and 16% said the listing looked abandoned or unclaimed. Every one of those is a checklist item below.

Here’s the whole list on one screen. Details follow.

How do you set up the parts that decide whether you show up?

Which category should you pick?

Google’s profile guidelines say to use the fewest categories that describe your core business, as specific as you can get. A good test: the category should finish the sentence “This business is a…” and not “This business has a…” Whitespark’s example is a law firm choosing “injury lawyer” over “law firm.” Same idea for you. “HVAC contractor” beats “contractor.”

Add a secondary category only for work you really do. Don’t add one because a competitor has it and you’d like their calls.

Does your name match your sign?

It has to. Google says to use your real-world name, as it appears on your storefront, website, and stationery. No taglines, hours, phone numbers, or service descriptions in it. “Smith Plumbing | Best Plumber Indianapolis” is the classic mistake, and Google says unnecessary information in the name could get your profile suspended. It’s tempting. Don’t.

Address, service area, and hours

Show your address only if customers come to you. If you drive to them, set a service area and hide the address. Google asks service-area businesses to keep one profile for the central location, and says service areas generally shouldn’t reach beyond about two hours of driving from your base. P.O. boxes and virtual offices aren’t eligible. One location, one profile.

Then hours. List your regular customer-facing hours and set special hours for holidays. We’ve seen more profiles say “open” on Thanksgiving than we’d like to count. Put a reminder on your calendar for early November.

The description, and the services list

You get 750 characters, according to BrightLocal’s guide to the field, and the start of it is what people see first. Say what you do and where in the first sentence. Write the way you’d talk across a counter. Google’s guidelines say no links of any kind, and no promotions, prices, or sales. Then list your services, one by one, in the words a customer would type.

Photos: what to add

Google’s photo guidelines are plain: JPG or PNG, between 10 KB and 5 MB, 720 by 720 pixels recommended and 250 by 250 as the minimum. Videos can run up to 30 seconds and 75 MB. Photos should look like your place. No heavy filters, and no stock.

Upload your logo, a clear shot of the outside so people can find the door, your team, and finished jobs. About ten now, then two or three a month. Your phone is good enough.

Posts and reviews

Posts are a small signal. Their real job is proof: a customer comparing two listings can see which business is active. Once a month, add a photo of recent work and two sentences.

Reviews feed prominence, and they need more room than this page has. The short version: text every customer your direct link after the job, ask everyone, offer nothing, reply to all of them. The routine, the rules, and a copy-ready text are in how to get more Google reviews.

What gets a profile suspended, and what doesn’t matter as much?

Per Google’s guidelines, the usual triggers are keywords or extra information in the business name, more than one profile for the same location, and breaking the content rules. We’d add one from experience: a profile an old web person or agency still owns. If someone else is the owner, ask them to add you as owner, then remove them.

What matters less than sellers say? Posting every day, for one. We think a monthly post is plenty. Chasing every optional attribute is another. And no checklist moves you closer to the searcher. A shop in Avon may never outrank one in Fishers for someone standing in Fishers, however polished the profile.

When can you skip this checklist?

If you sell only online, with no storefront and no service area, you may not be eligible for a profile at all. If you already finished everything above, run the free check below and look at your reviews instead. And if you have several locations or a suspended profile, that’s a job for a person who has done it before.

What else do owners ask about their Google profile?

How long does it take to optimize a Google Business Profile?

About an hour for the first pass if you have your photos and hours handy. After that, plan on ten minutes a month for a post and a few replies. Edits usually show up within a few days, once Google reviews them.

Can I put keywords in my Google Business Profile name?

No. Google says the name should match your real-world name, as on your storefront, website, and stationery, and warns that extra information such as taglines, phone numbers, or service descriptions could result in suspension.

Do I need a website to have a Google Business Profile?

No, a profile can exist without one. But BrightLocal’s 2026 study found 18% of consumers who passed on a business said they couldn’t find a website. A simple page that names your service and your town helps more than people expect.

How many categories should I choose?

One primary category, as specific as possible, and a secondary category only for work you really do. Google recommends the fewest categories that describe your core business. Whitespark’s 2026 survey ranks the primary category as the top local ranking factor.

Can I hide my address on Google?

Yes, if you visit customers instead of seeing them at your location. Google asks service-area businesses to hide their address and list the areas they serve. If customers come to you, show the address.
