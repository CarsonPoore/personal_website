Why isn’t my website getting leads?

How to tell a traffic problem from a conversion problem in 15 minutes, then five fixable causes with a self-check for each.

By Carson Poore Last updated: Oct 9, 2026

Either too few people arrive, or the people who arrive don’t act. Check your visits first. Low visits mean a being-found problem. Healthy visits mean one of five fixable causes: an unclear first screen, a buried contact path, a slow phone experience, thin proof, or leads that go cold after the form.

The good news is you can sort this out in about 15 minutes with tools you already have. Start with the table. Then work through the five causes in order.

Is it a traffic problem or a conversion problem?

They look the same from the outside. Both end with a quiet inbox. They need opposite fixes, so sort them first.

What you see Likely problem Start with
Few visits, few leads Traffic Cause 1
Plenty of visits, few leads Conversion Causes 2 and 3
Most visitors on phones, leads rare Phone experience Cause 4
Forms arrive, jobs don’t Follow-up, or the offer itself Cause 5
Leads were fine, then stopped Something broke Cause 5, then check for a drop in Google clicks

To fill in the first column, open Google Analytics (GA4) or your site builder’s stats and look at 90 days of visits. Then open Google Search Console and look at clicks from Google. Neither one set up? That’s step zero, and it’s free. Our analytics work covers the setup if you’d rather hand it off.

Then divide inquiries by visits. Unbounce’s 2024 benchmark puts the median landing page for commercial and professional services at 6.1% conversion. A whole site usually converts lower than a page built for one purpose, so don’t hold yourself to it. Use it as a loose reference, and watch your own number month to month.

The five usual causes, and a check for each

1. Not enough of the right people are arriving

A good site can’t create demand. If your 90-day visit count is a trickle, no redesign will change your inbox. Look at where visits come from. Search? Your Google profile? A link in an email? Then search for what you do, in your town, on your phone, and see whether you appear.

If you don’t, start with being found. Our guide to why a business isn’t showing up on Google walks through five free fixes. Come back here when the visits are steady.

2. The first screen doesn’t say what you do

Visitors decide fast. Nielsen Norman Group found that users often leave a page within 10 to 20 seconds, and the chance of leaving is highest in the first 10. A clever headline spends those seconds. A plain one saves them.

Plain language also converts. In that same Unbounce data, professional services pages written at a 5th to 7th grade reading level converted at a median of 12.9%, against 6.6% for pages at an 8th to 9th grade level. Short words win.

The check: show your homepage to someone outside your business for five seconds, then close it. Ask what you do and where you do it. If they can’t say, rewrite the first screen as one sentence with the service, the customer, and the town. Add a real photo of your work, a price or range if you can, and a recent review near the top. We think the words sell before the design does.

3. The next step is hard to find

A visitor who’s ready should never have to hunt. Put a tap-to-call number and one clear button on the first screen of every page. Keep the form short: a name, a phone number, and what they need. Every extra question gives someone a reason to quit.

The check: on your phone, start at the homepage and count the taps to reach a way to contact you. More than one means it’s buried. Then fill out your own form and see where it goes. Two minutes, no fun, and it catches more than you’d guess.

4. The site is slow or clumsy on a phone

Google’s guidance is that a page’s largest element should load in 2.5 seconds or less, measured on mobile. Slower than that, and a visitor on parking-lot signal is gone before the page finishes.

Phones matter more than owners expect. Unbounce found that for commercial and professional services pages, mobile made up 81% of visits but converted at 8.3%, against 11.6% on desktop. If your phone visitors convert worse than your desktop ones, the gap is often fixable.

The check: run your homepage through Google’s PageSpeed Insights, then turn off Wi-Fi and load it yourself on cellular. In our experience, giant photos and piled-up add-ons are the usual causes, and both are afternoon fixes.

5. Leads arrive, then go cold

Sometimes the website works and the inbox is where leads die. A form that sends to an old email address. Notifications landing in spam. A reply that takes two days. A 2011 Harvard Business Review study found that firms contacting a web lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify it as firms that waited even an hour longer. The study is old, and a lead that waits a day has often already called someone else.

The check: submit your own form at 9 p.m. Time how long until anyone replies. Look in the spam folder. Call your business number from another phone and see who picks up. If the answer is “tomorrow,” an automatic text that goes out within minutes is the cheapest fix we know. Our guide to AI agents in a small business shows how to do it with you approving the wording.

When the website isn’t the problem

Sometimes all five checks come back clean and the inbox is still quiet. Then the trouble is upstream. The offer isn’t clear yet. The price is out of line with the market. Or few people are looking for what you sell. A website can’t fix any of those, and we’d rather say so than sell you a new one. Our cost guide lists when a new website isn’t worth paying for.

And if you’ve fixed the basics and the site still limps, the next question is whether it needs a refresh or a full rebuild. We cover that in website redesign vs. refresh. Most sites need less than they think.

What else do owners ask about website leads?

How many leads should a small business website get?

There’s no honest universal number. It depends on your trade, your traffic, and your offer. Unbounce’s 2024 benchmark puts the median landing page for commercial and professional services at 6.1% conversion, but a whole site usually converts lower. Track visits and inquiries monthly and aim to beat your own last month.

How long until these fixes show up in my inbox?

Contact-path, speed, and first-screen fixes can show up within a few weeks of normal traffic, since they change what people do once they arrive. Fixes that bring more visitors, like Google profile and listing work, usually take a few months.

Is it my website or my marketing?

Check your visits first. If few people arrive, it’s a being-found problem, which is marketing. If plenty arrive and few contact you, the website, the follow-up, or the offer is the issue. The table above sorts the two.

Do I need a chatbot or a pop-up to get more leads?

Not first. Fix the first screen, the contact path, and your reply time before adding anything that talks to visitors. Our article on AI chatbots for small business websites covers when one is worth the risk.
