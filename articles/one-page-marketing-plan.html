How do I write a marketing plan for a small business?

A six-box template you can fill in over coffee, an example, and the signs that you need a second pair of eyes on it.

By Carson Poore Last updated: Oct 9, 2026

Use one page and six boxes: a goal with a number and a date, your best customer, your offer in one sentence, two channels, a weekly rhythm, and the one number you’ll check on day 90. Fill every box in about an hour. Then run it for 90 days before you change anything.

A marketing plan sounds like a binder. It doesn’t have to be. A page you’ll still read in week 7 beats a 30-page deck in a drawer, and you can fill this one in over a coffee.

Why does the plan need to fit on one page?

Because you have to be able to run it. The Small Business Administration agrees on the shape: its lean plan format is typically only one page and can take as little as an hour to make. A plan that short forces choices, and choices are the point.

There’s also evidence that vague goals are the common failure. In the Content Marketing Institute’s 2025 B2B research, 980 marketers answered, and among those who rated their strategy moderately effective or worse, 42% blamed a lack of clear goals. The top performers did the opposite: 47% said a documented strategy was part of why they succeeded. That’s B2B and self-rated, so treat it as a nudge. Small local businesses tend to have the same problem in a smaller costume.

What goes in a one-page marketing plan?

Six boxes. Here’s the whole template, with an example filled in for a made-up dog groomer in Fishers.

BoxThe question to answerExample
1. GoalWhat number, by what date?12 new regular clients by January 31.
2. Your peopleWho is the one best customer? Describe a real person.Doodle owners within 15 minutes of Fishers who rebook every 6 weeks.
3. Your offerWhat do you sell, and why choose you, in one sentence?Stress-free grooming for anxious dogs, with a text when your dog is ready.
4. Two channelsWhich two will you keep up for 90 days? What do they cost a month?Google profile with review requests, plus a monthly email to past clients. $150 a month.
5. Weekly rhythmWhat happens Monday, and for how many hours?Monday, 30 minutes: ask last week’s clients for reviews. Thursday, 1 hour: one photo, one post, one email draft.
6. The checkWhich one number do you look at, and when?New clients per week, every Friday. Full review on day 90.

Print it. Tape it somewhere you’ll see it. If a box takes you longer than ten minutes, that’s useful information, and we’ll get to it below.

What does a plan with too much in it look like?

Picture the same groomer writing “post on Instagram, TikTok, and LinkedIn, run Google Ads, start a newsletter, and try a podcast.” That’s a wish list. Nobody has the hours, and a month later none of it is working because none of it got enough attention.

The fix is to cut until it hurts. Keep the two channels closest to where the customer already is, and park the rest in a “maybe next quarter” note. The parked list isn’t failure. It’s the reason you can focus.

How do you pick a goal you can check?

Put a number and a date in it. “Get more customers” can’t fail, so it can’t tell you anything. “12 new regular clients by January 31” can fail, and that’s why it helps.

Be honest about what you’d compare it against. In vcita’s 2025 survey of small businesses, 60% of the owners who dropped an agency cited a lack of return. That’s hard to judge when the plan never named a number to begin with. The fix fits in box 6: one number, checked on a schedule.

How do you choose only two channels?

Start where people already trust you. Nielsen’s 2021 trust study found that 88% of respondents trusted recommendations from friends over other kinds of promotion. That’s a reason to start with reviews and referrals before you buy anything.

Then pick two, not five. We think two channels done every week beat five done when you remember. It’s also easier to learn something. If a channel is the only thing that changed, you know what to credit when the number moves. Give each one at least eight weeks before you judge it.

For the groomer, that’s the Google profile and an email to past clients, because her best customers already search for “groomer near me” and already know her name. A founder selling to other companies would pick differently, likely direct outreach plus one online community. The test is the same either way. Where is your best customer already looking, and can you show up there every week?

Hours matter more than owners expect. Fiverr’s survey of nearly 6,000 owners found 70% spend less than five hours a week on marketing. Write your real number in box 5 and build around it. A plan that needs 10 hours when you have 3 is a wish.

When should you change the plan?

Not in week three. Run it for the full 90 days, then review it. Look at the one number from box 6, keep what moved it, and drop what didn’t. Boring, yes. It’s also where the learning is.

The exception is a fact that changes the plan, like a shop closing in your service area or a busy season starting early. Change the plan for facts. Don’t change it for boredom.

Who doesn’t need a plan this formal?

A solo owner with a full calendar and plenty of referrals. If the phone rings and you’re turning work away, write down your goal and your two channels, and move on. A one-pager is overkill when your problem is capacity, not demand.

What if you get stuck on a box?

Boxes 2 and 3 are the ones people stall on: who it’s really for, and what makes you the obvious choice. That’s the signal. Those two answers hold up everything else, and they’re easier to find with an outside voice asking questions.

What else do owners ask about marketing plans?

How long should a small business marketing plan be?

One page is enough for most small businesses. The SBA’s lean plan format is typically one page. If you can’t fit it on a page, the plan probably has too many goals or too many channels.

How often should I update my marketing plan?

Review it every 90 days, and rewrite it at the same time. Check your one number weekly. Don’t swap channels before they’ve had about eight weeks, unless a fact has changed, like a closed location or a new competitor nearby.

What’s the difference between a marketing plan and a marketing strategy?

Strategy is who you serve and why they should pick you. The plan is what you’ll do about it, when, and how much time it takes. You need the strategy first, because a plan built on a vague strategy only keeps you busy.

Do I need a budget in my marketing plan?
