## 1. Local small businesses

### Snapshot

Owner-operated businesses in Indianapolis and the surrounding towns: HVAC and plumbing contractors, remodelers, restaurants, independent retail, salons. Revenue between $250K and $5M. The buyer is the owner, full stop. She answers the phone, runs the crew or the floor, and did the marketing herself on nights and weekends until she couldn't. Nobody on staff has "marketing" in their title.

### What's true about their world right now

2026 is a squeeze year, and they feel it before the economists do.

- Indianapolis metro GDP growth is projected at just 1.5–2.0% for 2026, with unemployment drifting above 4% and named pullbacks in consumer spending (Indiana Business Research Center).
- Inflation returned as the single most important problem for small business owners by mid-2026, the highest reading since October 2024, and a net negative 9% of owners reported higher sales (NFIB).
- Tariffs hit Indiana trades directly: steel, aluminum, and copper costs up 30–50% for US buyers (Indiana SBA economic outlook; IBRC).
- Meanwhile the agency industry keeps burning them. A vcita survey of 500 US SMB owners found 40% discontinue or switch agencies, 56% of that churn within 6–12 months, and 60% citing lack of ROI. An UpCity survey found 80% of departing clients cite poor communication.

So every dollar is scrutinized, and the last people who took their marketing dollars gave them charts. That is the room we walk into.

### Pains, in their own words

- "The phones just stopped ringing."
- "Word of mouth has carried us for 15 years, but it's not enough anymore."
- "I paid them a thousand a month and I honestly couldn't tell you what they did."
- "My competitor shows up first on Google and we don't show up at all."
- "Angi leads are garbage — same lead gets sold to five other guys."
- "I need booked jobs, not likes."
- "They talked over my head — impressions, CTR, 'the algorithm' — just tell me straight."
- "How do I know you're not like the last company?"
- "We're busy, but we're not growing — same customers, same revenue, more costs."

### Buying triggers

In observed order of frequency: (1) phones and walk-ins slow down and word of mouth stops being enough; (2) a competitor visibly outranks them on Google or has a line out the door; (3) revenue plateaus despite working harder; (4) owner burnout — they DIY'd the marketing and can't anymore; (5) they are recovering from a bad agency or lead-platform experience. Seasonality matters: the Q1 trades slow season and the post-holiday restaurant dip are the highest-receptivity windows.

### Objections and how we pre-empt them

| Objection | Pre-emption |
|---|---|
| "I got burned before. How are you different?" | Three months minimum, then month to month. You own everything — site, Google Business Profile, ad accounts — and keep it all if you leave. References you can call. |
| "I can't tell if marketing works." | A monthly plain-English report counting calls, booked jobs, and dollars. Never impressions. Call tracking from day one. |
| "That's a lot of money right now." | Most businesses this size already spend $500–$3,000/mo on marketing (diymarketers survey), often renting leads from Angi. We price against one empty crew-day, not against zero. |
| "I don't have time for this." | One 30-minute call a month. That's the owner's whole time cost. |
| "You won't understand my business." | The first thing we ask is average job value, service area, and best customers — the questions the last agency never asked. |

### What they buy first, and the pathway

Entry is a Marketing retainer, led by local visibility: Google Business Profile, reviews, local SEO. It is the highest-trust, most explainable early win — BrightLocal data puts businesses in the local 3-pack at 126% more traffic than positions 4–10, and listings with 40+ reviews at a 4.5+ average appear in the 3-pack about 3.7x more often. From there: speed-to-lead automation from the **Technical** practice ("every call, text, and form answered in minutes, even when you're on a roof"), then Google Ads done honestly — own your leads instead of renting them. The $3,000 **Strategy** engagement is the step-up for owners who ask "what should we do first," and **Coaching & growth** fits the rare owner building toward a real marketing function. We do not lead with strategy or branding here. These owners buy done-for-you consistency and visible results.

### Red flags — who we turn away

- Owners who want a "double your leads in 30 days" promise. We won't make it, and whoever will is lying to them.
- Businesses with a broken operation behind the marketing: phones that go unanswered, review scores earned honestly. Marketing a leaky bucket wastes their money.
- Anyone who wants ads before their Google profile and review base exist. We tell them what to fix first, free, and ask them to come back.
- Price-shoppers hunting below $1,200/mo. The cap is eight clients; the math only works when the engagement is real.

### The message (verbatim, from the messaging map)

> Word of mouth built your business; it just stopped scaling. When Indianapolis searches for what you do, you'll show up — and every call, text, and form gets answered in minutes. Published prices, three months then month to month, monthly reports that count calls and booked jobs, and you keep everything if you ever leave. We'll also tell you what not to buy — starting with ads you're not ready for.

## 2. Founders & startups

### Snapshot

Early-stage founders: pre-seed to seed, plus bootstrapped SaaS and service founders, from first dollar to roughly $2M ARR. Local founders live in the Indianapolis ecosystem — High Alpha offices, gBETA pitch nights, Elevate Ventures portfolios — and the rest are anywhere, because this work travels. The buyer is the founder personally, usually the technical or product one, which is exactly the problem.

### What's true about their world right now

- Capital efficiency is the mood. Indianapolis startups raised roughly $892M across about 147 deals in 2025 (superscout.co), and TechPoint's venture report described the year as recalibration and renewed focus on long-term value creation. Founders are spending carefully.
- Founder-led sales breaks around $1–2M ARR — more precisely, when the founder personally closes 70–80% of new business and burns 15+ hours a week doing it (SaaStr).
- Agency skepticism is structural. SaaStr's canonical line: hiring a bunch of agencies instead of a marketing leader never works. Cervin Ventures calls the early-stage agency relationship structurally set up to produce the wrong outcomes.
- Full-time CMOs cost too much for this stage and don't stay — fractional CMOs typically run 40–50% less than a full-time hire, against an average CMO tenure around 4.1 years (2026 market coverage via data-mania.com, marketful.com). Typical fractional rates run $5,000–$15,000 a month. Ours run $1,200–$4,800.

The recurring diagnosis across sources: most startups don't stall because the product doesn't work. They stall because the messaging is unclear, sales is guessing, and nobody agrees who the product is for. Positioning problems masquerade as growth problems.

### Pains, in their own words

- "I built it and nobody came."
- "Distribution was the real problem all along."
- "Getting the first 100 users is so much harder than building the app."
- "Founder-led sales is breaking — the company only grows when I personally get on the call."
- "We tried an agency and got reports, not revenue."
- "We're doing random acts of marketing."
- "Nobody's aligned on who this is actually for."
- "I know we need marketing but I can't afford a real CMO."
- "I need someone who's done this at my stage, not a Fortune 500 resume."
- "I need a repeatable playbook I can eventually hand to a hire."

### Buying triggers

(1) Launch lands flat — the distribution problem becomes undeniable; (2) founder-led sales hits its hour ceiling; (3) an agency engagement ends in reports and no customers; (4) a raise or revenue milestone forces the "who actually buys this" question; (5) a peer founder says "here's who I'd hire again." That last one is the channel. Founders evaluate consultants through peer referrals and stage-matched proof before anything else.

### Objections and how we pre-empt them

| Objection | Pre-emption |
|---|---|
| "We got burned by an agency." | An agency owns deliverables. We own outcomes — revenue, a GTM plan, a channel that repeats. The 8-client cap is the structural proof of attention. |
| "Are we too early for this?" | Maybe. If you're pre-product-market fit, don't buy ads or a retainer from anyone. The strategy engagement tells you what to do yourself first — and if even that is premature, we say so and send you off with homework. |
| "Why not just hire someone?" | A full-time marketer at this stage costs more and still needs direction. Buy the playbook first; hire against it later. The #1 failure mode of the first marketing hire is a founder who never documented a repeatable process (SaaStr). |
| "Have you worked with founders at my stage?" | We show stage-specific proof before being asked, and where founder case studies don't exist yet, we bridge honestly from real local and nonprofit work plus cited industry data. We don't invent results. |

### What they buy first, and the pathway

Entry is the $3,000 **Strategy** engagement — positioning, actual ICP, a sharp offer, a first repeatable channel, a 90-day plan the founder could run alone. That deliverable sits exactly at the verified market price for positioning/GTM sprints ($1,500–$3,000+, per Contra, Maven, columncontent.com). Continuation is **Coaching & growth**: fractional CMO at well under category rates, always "fractional CMO / marketing advisor," never coach-speak. **Technical** is the wedge agencies can't match — lead qualification and outreach automation, AI chat support, onboarding automation, GA4 instrumentation — framed as the ops work that stops the founder being the bottleneck. **Marketing** execution comes last, once the strategy says which channel earns it.

### Red flags — who we turn away

- Pre-PMF founders asking for paid media. Ads are for scale, not for starting. We say so and decline the retainer, which is also how this audience learns to trust us.
- Founders shopping for a growth miracle on a dying product. Positioning can't fix a thing nobody wants.
- Anyone who wants a vendor to blame rather than a playbook to own. The engagement ends with the founder able to run it.
- Stage mismatch upward: a Series B company needing a full demand-gen team should hire one. We'll say that in the first call.

### The message (verbatim, from the messaging map)

> You built the thing; now we get it to people. Start with the $3,000 strategy engagement — positioning, who it's actually for, a first repeatable channel — then keep us on as your fractional CMO at well under category rates. We think like owners, own outcomes instead of deliverables, and build the automation too. If you're pre-product-market fit, we'll say so and send you off with homework instead of a retainer.

## 3. Nonprofits

### Snapshot

Community-sized nonprofits in central Indiana with budgets from $100K to $5M. The buyer is an executive director or development director who is also the marketer, the volunteer coordinator, and sometimes the bookkeeper. Decisions pass through a board that scrutinizes anything that smells like overhead. They don't hire from RFPs for engagements this size; they interview two or three referred consultants and choose on trust and sector fluency more than price (Nonprofit Times; ASU Lodestar).

### What's true about their world right now

- Dollars up, donors down: 2025 Fundraising Effectiveness Project data shows total giving up about 5% while donor count fell 3.6%, continuing a decline every year since 2019. The smallest donors ($1–$100) shrank 11.1% year over year — exactly the base community-sized orgs depend on.
- 81% of nonprofits surveyed in 2025 struggled to raise enough to cover costs; 36% ended the year with an operating deficit (Independent Sector).
- The Google Ad Grant — up to $10,000 a month in free search ads for verified 501(c)(3)s — is confirmed active in 2026. The average self-managed account uses only about $300 of it, and industry-reported figures put professionally managed accounts near $8,650 a month. Accounts get suspended constantly over the 5% click-through rule and the monthly-login rule.
- Donor data lives in spreadsheets, an email tool, a payment processor, and an event platform that don't talk to each other; nonprofit email still earns about $2.40 per subscriber per year and roughly 11% of online revenue (M+R Benchmarks 2025).
- Indiana is unusually funder-rich: Lilly Endowment's GIFT IX put up to $271.5M into community foundations, a community foundation serves every one of Indiana's 92 counties, and United Way of Central Indiana runs capacity-building grants up to $50,000 — money that can pay for exactly this kind of help.

### Pains, in their own words

- "We're wearing too many hats — there's no one here whose actual job is marketing."
- "The board wants to see every dollar go to programs, not overhead."
- "We have the Google Grant but honestly nobody's touched it in months."
- "Our donor data is in three different spreadsheets and nobody trusts any of them."
- "Donor fatigue is real — our year-end appeal did less than last year."
- "We need help telling our story — people don't understand what we actually do."
- "Our development director just left and everything lived in her head."
- "We rely on volunteers, but recruiting them is harder every year."
- "Is this something a capacity-building grant could cover?"
- "We don't want a vendor, we want someone who gets the nonprofit world."

### Buying triggers

(1) A year-end or GivingTuesday appeal underperforms; (2) the Ad Grant gets suspended, or someone discovers it was never activated; (3) a development director leaves and the institutional knowledge walks out with them; (4) the board asks for numbers the ED can't produce; (5) a capacity-building grant window opens and suddenly there is money for help. The volunteer pipeline is a co-equal trigger most agencies ignore: 75% of nonprofits say volunteers are essential, and volunteering has been sliding for a decade (Census/AmeriCorps via ActivateGood; BLS).

### Objections and how we pre-empt them

| Objection | Pre-emption |
|---|---|
| "The board will see this as overhead." | Marketing spend is mission delivery: more donors found, more volunteers recruited, more people served. We give the ED the sentence to say in the board meeting, and dashboards the board can actually read. |
| "There's no money for capacity." | There may already be. United Way of Central Indiana's Capacity Fund awards grants up to $50,000, and a community foundation serves every Indiana county. We'll help write the capacity-grant case for this engagement. |
| "We've had vendors who didn't get the sector." | Restricted funds, stewardship, lapsed donors, board dynamics — the copy and the conversation prove the fluency rather than claim it. |
| "We can't afford consultant rates." | Nonprofits get a reduced rate. Not because the work is smaller — because the budgets are, and the work matters. Same scope, same cap, same three-month minimum as every client. |

One opinion we hold: the "mission rate" framing matters more than the number. The sector resents charity framing and responds to partnership. We never say "discount" and never say "giving back."

### What they buy first, and the pathway

Entry is a free Ad Grant checkup: eligible? active? suspended? how much of the $10,000 was used last month? It is low-risk, concrete, and most orgs discover they are leaving thousands of free ad dollars unspent monthly. From there, in order: Ad Grants setup and management (**Marketing** + **Technical**), one donor list everyone trusts — fixing the three-spreadsheets problem with CRM and email cleanup (**Technical**) — then storytelling and the case for support (**Marketing**), then GA4 and simple board-readable dashboards (**Technical**). The $3,000 **Strategy** engagement fits neatly inside a capacity-grant application, and **Coaching & growth** serves EDs who need a standing marketing advisor more than hands. Automation is sold as "a small team that runs like a bigger one," never as replacing the human relationships that drive giving.

### Red flags — who we turn away

- Orgs that want marketing to paper over a program or governance problem. More donors won't fix a mission the board can't articulate.
- Boards that demand attribution certainty down to the dollar before approving anything. We report honestly; we don't manufacture precision.
- Faith-based or community orgs we can't verify for Google for Nonprofits eligibility — we check during the audit rather than promise on the page, and we say "faith-based and community organizations welcome" because they are.
- Anyone shopping for free work beyond the checkup. The reduced rate is respect, and it is still a rate.

### The message (verbatim, from the messaging map)

> You're wearing five hats and marketing falls to whoever has ten spare minutes. Start with a free Ad Grant checkup — Google offers verified nonprofits $10,000 a month in free ads, and most orgs use a few hundred dollars of it — then one donor list everyone trusts, stories people understand, and numbers your board can read. The nonprofit rate isn't charity: the budgets are smaller and the work matters.

## How to use this document

- **Pathway pages** (/local-business, /founders, /nonprofits): open with the pains section in the reader's own words, answer the objections table on the page, and lead with the entry offer named above for that segment.
- **Sales conversations**: the red-flags lists are real qualification criteria, not posture. Turning away a bad fit early is cheaper for everyone and is the single strongest trust signal each of these audiences names.
- **Content**: every language-bank line is a candidate headline or article topic. The buying triggers are the editorial calendar.
- **What never ships**: invented client numbers or testimonials, unverified market-size figures, "business coach" language, charity framing for the nonprofit rate, and promises tied to a timeline we can't control.
