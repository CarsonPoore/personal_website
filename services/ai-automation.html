The short answer

What an AI consultant does, and what it costs.

An AI automation consultant builds software that takes one repeated job off your plate. Answering new leads. Asking intake questions. Sending review requests. Pulling Monday’s numbers. A good one also tells you which jobs to leave alone.

Independent AI consultants typically charge $150 to $250 an hour, and a defined small-business project usually runs $1,500 to $6,000, according to published rate guides from Bushe, Rapid Developers, and Kamyar Shah.

We price this the way we price everything. Ongoing automation work sits inside our published retainers of $1,200, $2,400, or $4,800 a month, with a three-month minimum. A one-off build gets a fixed quote in writing before any work starts. Nonprofits get the reduced rate listed on the pricing page. If you ever leave, every automation and account we built stays with you, along with your data.

What we build

Five jobs worth handing to an agent.

When vcita surveyed 500 small-business owners in 2025, the AI help they asked for was refreshingly plain: content, and fast replies to inquiries. Our list looks a lot like theirs. Each job below shows what the agent does and what a person still does. Both columns matter.

The job What the agent does What a person does
01

Lead follow-up in minutes

The agent

A form or a missed call comes in. Within minutes the lead gets a reply that answers the question they actually asked, plus two open times from your real calendar. Speed matters more than polish here.

A person

Approves the wording once. Anything off-script, like a complaint or a big commercial bid, waits for a tap.

Intake without phone tag

The agent

Asks the same dozen questions you would, by text or on a form that talks back. It collects the address and photos, then books the visit.

A person

03

Review requests

The agent

The day a job closes, it sends one text with one link to your Google profile. One reminder. Then it stops.

Reporting you don’t assemble

The agent

Every Monday at 7 a.m., it pulls calls, booked jobs, ad spend, and new reviews onto one page. No more five logins on a Sunday night.

A person

Reads it in two minutes. Makes the decision it points to.

05

Content drafts

The agent

Drafts the monthly customer email and the quote follow-up you keep forgetting, written from your real work in your voice.

A person

Edits, then publishes. Nothing posts itself. Ever.

Where we stand

Your name is on it, so you sign off.

Here’s our opinion, plainly. An agent should sort and draft on its own, and anything a customer reads gets a human yes first.

Some AI shops sell full autopilot because it demos well. We think that’s backwards for a small business. One strange automated text to a ten-year customer costs more trust than a hundred fast replies earn back. Approval takes a single tap on your phone, so you keep the speed.

How we start

One automation first. Then we talk.

Every engagement opens with a scoping pass. We list the tasks you repeat and put your own hours next to each one. Then we pick the single build where being slow costs you real money. Usually that’s lead follow-up. It ships on software you already pay for, then runs for a few weeks with you approving everything before we propose a second build.

Now the candid part. Plenty of AI projects shouldn’t exist. The same rate guides that publish consultant prices pass along a sobering pattern: most small-business AI engagements don’t pay for themselves in the first year (as reported by Rapid Developers and Kamyar Shah). The usual cause is building something big before anyone mapped the chore. So sometimes our scoping verdict is “keep the spreadsheet,” and we’d rather say that than bill you for a robot.

One more admission. The first week of any agent is a little clumsy. Expect to correct it. That’s what the approval step is for.

Worth building when
  • You do the task the same way almost every time.
  • Being slow costs money. Lead follow-up is the classic case.
  • Someone can check the output in seconds.
  • The information already lives in a tool you own.
Don’t hire anyone for it (us included) when
  • It happens twice a month. A checklist is cheaper.
  • Nobody will own the approvals. Unwatched automations drift.
  • The real problem is your offer or your price. No agent fixes that, and we’ll tell you so on the call.

Fair questions

Before you hand anything to a machine.

How much does an AI automation consultant cost for a small business?

Independent consultants typically charge $150 to $250 an hour, and a defined small-business project usually runs $1,500 to $6,000, per published rate guides from Bushe, Rapid Developers, and Kamyar Shah. With us, ongoing automation work is part of the published monthly retainer: $1,200, $2,400, or $4,800, with a three-month minimum. A one-off build gets a fixed written quote before any work starts.

What can an AI agent actually do in a five-person business?

Less than the ads suggest, and that’s fine. The dependable jobs are replying to new leads within minutes, running intake by text or form, sending review requests, assembling a weekly report, and drafting emails and posts. Each one works best on a task you already do the same way every day.

Will the AI send messages to my customers without me seeing them?

Not one we build. You approve the wording of routine replies up front, and anything unusual waits for a person to tap approve. Content drafts never publish on their own. If you want to loosen that rule later, it’s your call.

Do I need to buy new software first?

Usually not. We build on the inbox and calendar you already use. If your leads are scattered across texts and sticky notes, the better first move may be a proper CRM, and we’ll say so. That’s a GoHighLevel setup, and it has its own page.

Do you work with businesses outside Indianapolis?

Yes. We’re based in Indianapolis, and the AI and automation practice is national. Builds happen over calls and shared screens, so a shop in Fishers and a startup in Denver get the same process.
